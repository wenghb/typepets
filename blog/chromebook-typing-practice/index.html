Typing Practice on a Chromebook: A Guide for Kids, Parents & Teachers

September 23, 2026 · 5 min read

For many kids, a Chromebook is the first "real" computer they type on, often a school device that comes home in a backpack. The good news: the letter keys are in exactly the same places as on any other QWERTY keyboard, so everything a child learns about touch typing transfers directly. But Chromebooks do have a few quirks that can trip up young typists. Here's what to know, plus tips for school devices and keeping progress safe.

How a Chromebook keyboard is different

1. The Search (or Launcher) key sits where Caps Lock usually is

On most keyboards, the key to the left of A is Caps Lock. On a Chromebook it's the Search or Launcher key (it may show a magnifying glass, a circle, or a newer "Everything" symbol). This matters for touch typing, because that key sits right next to the left pinky's home key.

When a beginner's pinky slips, the launcher opens and grabs the keyboard. The next few letters go into the search box instead of the lesson. If that happens:

  • Press Esc (or the same key again) to close the launcher.
  • Click back inside the typing lesson and carry on.
  • Practice "anchoring" the left pinky on A. The pinky should reach up for Q, down for Z, and left only for Tab and Shift.

Need capitals? Touch typists use Shift (held with the opposite hand's pinky) rather than Caps Lock anyway. If a child really needs Caps Lock, on most Chromebooks it's Search + Alt (or Launcher + Alt).

Want to change it? ChromeOS keyboard settings let you change what the Search/Launcher key does, for example making it act as Caps Lock. The exact menu names vary between ChromeOS versions, and on school-managed devices this setting may be locked.

2. There's no F1–F12 row

The top row holds shortcut keys instead: back, refresh, full screen, window overview, brightness, and volume. Typing lessons don't use F-keys, so this doesn't matter for learning, but one key deserves a warning: refresh. A child reaching clumsily for the number row can reload the page mid-exercise. That's one more reason to practice the number row carefully once the letters are solid. (If you ever need a real F-key, hold Search and press the top-row key.)

3. Small differences that come up later

  • There's usually no Delete key. Alt + Backspace deletes forward.
  • Home and End are Search + Left arrow and Search + Right arrow.
  • Ctrl + Alt + / (forward slash) opens a keyboard-shortcut helper, which is handy for curious kids.

Setting up for comfortable practice

Chromebooks are compact, and the touchpad sits right under the wrists. Two common problems follow from that:

  • Palms brushing the touchpad can move the cursor or click somewhere else, pulling focus out of the lesson. Teach kids to keep their wrists slightly raised and hovering, not resting on the touchpad. Lowering touchpad sensitivity or using a plug-in mouse also helps.
  • Hunching over a low screen. If you can, put the Chromebook on a stand or a stack of books and use an external keyboard. If not, sit back, feet flat, elbows roughly level with the keyboard, and eyes on the screen.

Tips for school Chromebooks

  • Use classroom links. Teachers can share a link that opens a specific lesson with a timer, such as typepets.com/pages/training.html?stage=1&min=10. Post it in your LMS or Google Classroom and students land directly in the right exercise. See TypePets for teachers to build one.
  • Headphones or mute. In a full classroom, either use headphones for sound effects and spoken finger hints, or turn sound off.
  • Shorter, more frequent sessions. Ten focused minutes three to five times a week beats one long session. Typing warm-ups at the start of a lesson are an easy way to fit this in.
  • Eyes on the screen. Some teachers place a folded piece of card over students' hands once they know the home row, as a gentle nudge to stop looking down.

Keeping progress safe on managed Chromebooks

This part is easy to miss. Many kid-friendly tools, TypePets included, save progress in the browser on the device rather than in an online account. That's what makes them private, but school Chromebooks add some wrinkles:

  • Shared carts: progress stays on the specific Chromebook (and signed-in profile) where it was made. A different Chromebook tomorrow starts fresh.
  • Data wiped at sign-out: some schools set Chromebooks to clear all local data when a student signs out, or have students use guest mode. On those devices, progress disappears at the end of every session.
  • Browser cleanup: clearing browsing data or site data also clears saved progress.

The fix: a backup code. In TypePets this is the Pet Passport, a save code that holds a child's typing progress. At the end of a session, copy the code and keep it somewhere that follows the student, like a document in their school Drive. Next time, paste it in to restore the pet and progress on any Chromebook. Treat it like a game save: nothing breaks if it's lost, but it's much nicer to keep.

For teachers, a simple routine works well: "Last minute of typing: update your Pet Passport." Students on personal, non-wiped Chromebooks can skip this and keep their progress automatically.

How much practice?

For most kids aged 7–12, 10–15 minutes a day is plenty. Accuracy comes first; speed follows. If you're wondering what's realistic at each age, see our typing goals by grade.

The bottom line

Chromebooks are great typing machines once kids know two things: keep the left pinky away from the Search key, and don't worry about the missing F-keys. On school devices, add one habit, backing up progress, and the Chromebook becomes a perfectly good place to learn touch typing.
