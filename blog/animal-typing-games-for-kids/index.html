Animal Typing Games for Kids: Learn to Type With a Pet

October 22, 2026 · 6 min read

Ask a group of seven-year-olds what they'd like to type and you'll hear "dog", "shark" and "unicorn" long before you hear "the quick brown fox". Animals are one of the easiest ways to get kids to want to sit down at a keyboard. But a lot of "animal typing games" are just letters falling past cartoon animals, and they quietly teach kids to hunt and peck with two fingers.

This guide covers what makes an animal typing game actually teach typing, how a virtual pet keeps kids practicing, and ready-made animal word lists sorted by keyboard row, so the words match what your child has learned so far.

Why animals work so well

  • A reason to come back tomorrow. Typing is built by short, regular practice, not one long session. A pet that is hungry, happy or about to grow gives kids a reason to open the game again the next day.
  • Mistakes feel low-stakes. Missing a bubble with a friendly animal on screen feels like part of a game, not a test.
  • Kids already know the words. Animal names are short, familiar and easy to read, so young typists can focus on their fingers instead of the spelling.

What to look for in an animal typing game

The animal is the hook. What matters is what happens under it:

  • It teaches finger placement. Look for lessons that start on the home row (A S D F and J K L ;) and add one row at a time. If a game only asks kids to hit keys as fast as possible, they'll fall back on two fingers. Our finger placement guide shows the full map.
  • Practice is the only way to earn rewards. The pet should grow because your child typed, not because someone bought gems or watched an ad. Avoid games with a shop, loot boxes or a second currency.
  • Missing a day isn't a disaster. Kids have sick days and busy weekends. A pet that dies or a streak that resets to zero turns practice into guilt. Gentle is better.
  • Accuracy counts. Good games reward getting keys right, not just going fast. Speed follows accuracy.
  • No ads and no chat. For young kids, ads pull attention away from the lesson, and open chat with strangers isn't needed to learn to type. Our checklist for typing games with no ads and no login goes into more detail.
  • Short sessions. 10 to 15 minutes a day is plenty for most kids aged 7 to 12.

One-hand animals

In touch typing, the left hand covers Q W E R T, A S D F G and Z X C V B, and the right hand covers the rest. A few animals can be typed with one hand only, which makes a fun challenge:

  • Left hand only: cat, bee, rat, bat, bear, deer, crab, zebra, beaver, ferret
  • Right hand only: lion, hippo, puppy

Four animal typing activities

  1. Zoo roll call. Pick ten animals from the right list and type each one three times, eyes on the screen. Count how many came out right the first time.
  2. Animal alphabet. Type one animal for every letter from A to Z: ant, bear, cat, dog… X is the tricky one, so "fox" (with an X at the end) counts.
  3. Pet story. Have your child type three sentences about their real or virtual pet: what it eats, where it sleeps, and its funniest habit. It's real writing practice in disguise.
  4. One-hand race. Type the one-hand animals above, keeping the other hand on its home keys the whole time. It's harder than it sounds.

Frequently asked questions

Are animal typing games good for learning to type?

Yes, as long as the game teaches which finger presses which key and rewards accuracy. The animals keep kids coming back; the lessons underneath do the teaching. A game that only measures speed tends to reinforce hunt-and-peck.

What age are animal typing games for?

Most work best for ages 7 to 12, when hands are big enough to rest on the home row. Younger children can still enjoy finding letters and typing their name, but don't worry about proper touch typing before about age 7. See our typing goals by grade for what's realistic at each age.

The bottom line

An animal is a great reason to practice, but only if the practice is real. Look for a game that starts on the home row, rewards accuracy, and lets the pet grow only through typing. Then add a few animal words from the lists above, and ten minutes a day starts to feel like playtime.
